\begin{abstract}
Trusted monitoring in AI control is evaluated on stored honest/attack pairs. The field
already knows these pairs leak: it filters to working solutions, strips comments, and
states why. We ask what that preprocessing leaves behind. On the field's own filtered,
comment-stripped split, a bag-of-words classifier scores above roughly four-fifths of the
open-weight monitors ranked on that same split, and a classifier that reads no code at
all --- only problem metadata --- scores above most of them as well. We trace the residue
to authorship rather than sabotage: the honest class is human-written while every attack
is model-written. The signal transfers almost unchanged across backdoor generators, is as
strong on inert backdoors as on working ones, and survives the field's own comment
stripper in the form of author bylines left inside string literals. Five same-generator
agentic settings, where no authorship confound is possible, carry a second and structural
floor ranging from chance to strongly leaky. Regenerating only the honest half does not
isolate content: with an open-weight model writing honest solutions against the stored
backdoors, separability \emph{rises} rather than falls, because the substitute generator
introduces a larger authorship gap than it removes. We therefore hold authorship constant
by construction, generating both classes from a single model, and report what a
bag-of-words classifier can still separate. We recommend that stored-pair monitor
evaluations report partial-input and bag-of-words floors against permutation nulls, use
same-generator honest samples, and drop inert backdoors from both classes.
\end{abstract}

Table~\ref{tab:schema} is the argument of this paper in a form that requires no
measurement. It is the record schema of \texttt{RoganInglis/apps-control-arena}
\cite{inglis2025appscontrolarena}, the canonical stored-pair artifact for APPS-based
control evaluations. The attack class carries \texttt{model} and \texttt{source}, naming
the system that produced each backdoor. The honest class carries no such field --- not
because it was omitted, but because there is no generator to name. Honest solutions are
taken from APPS \cite{hendrycks2021apps} and are human-written; no model is called at any
point in their construction. Every backdoor is model-written.

\paragraph{Artifacts and provenance.}
We use seven public corpora, and their per-class provenance is the object of study rather
than an assumption: in the APPS family the honest class is human and the attack class is one
of ten named models, while in the five agentic settings the same three models write
\emph{both} classes. \textbf{Two of the seven declare no licence}
\cite{koran2026highquality, sinha2026controlarenacorpora}; we report aggregates, redistribute
nothing, and say the licences are absent rather than implying they are permissive. We exclude
AgentHarm, whose author is an invited speaker here. Note also that the APPS-backdoor corpora
are two views of one artifact, not two datasets: every problem in the filtered release
\cite{koran2026highquality} is present in \cite{inglis2025appscontrolarena}, byte-identical
except a re-validated \texttt{backdoor\_works}.

\paragraph{Evaluation surface, and naming protocols.}
Our headline protocol is the filtered, comment-stripped split of \cite{koran2026ensemble}:
\splitTrain{} train and \splitTest{} test items (\splitAttack{} attack, \splitHonest{}
honest), labelled by \texttt{backdoor\_works}, reconstructed with the released code of
\cite{jha2026decorrelation} rather than our own. It is the surface on which \monN{}
open-weight monitors have published per-item scores, which is what makes a floor comparable
to a monitor at all. The raw artifact, this filtered split, pooled evaluation, matched pairs
and within-task evaluation are five different populations, and pairwise accuracy and AUROC
are two different metrics; every comparison below names its split, metric and preprocessing,
and no value is placed beside a count from another protocol. The failure is not
hypothetical --- an earlier version of this work compared a problem-level floor against a
pairwise accuracy on a matched protocol where that floor is $0.5000$ by construction.

\paragraph{Test harness.}
Gate S requires executing generated code, so we built a runner that feeds each shipped input
case to a solution under per-case timeouts and address-space limits. It agrees with the
artifact's own \texttt{solution\_passes\_tests} on \harnessAgreement{} of the analysis pool,
and we use \emph{our own} flag for both classes: a checker stricter on one class than the
other would manufacture exactly the artifact this paper documents.

\section{Results}
% Budget: 1.5 pages - the paper's weight. OUTLINE.md section 3.

% --- F1: the protocol x baseline table. Generated by experiments/apps/figures.py. ----
\input{fig/f1-protocol-baseline}

Table~\ref{tab:protocol-baseline} reports what a deliberately trivial classifier recovers
under each protocol. The single most informative pair of rows is the contrast between
\lenFiltered{} and \tfidfKoran{}. Code length --- the carrier the field's preprocessing was
aimed at --- is driven to chance on the filtered split, exactly as intended, and the
\texttt{\#}-count detector likewise falls from \hashRaw{} to \hashStripped{}. The fix
works. But on that same filtered, comment-stripped split a bag-of-words classifier still
reaches \tfidfKoran{}, above \tfidfKoranCount{} of the \monN{} monitors ranked there, and a
classifier that reads \emph{no code at all} --- only problem difficulty, test count and
question length --- reaches \nocodeCV{}, above \nocodeCVCount{}. The preprocessing removed
the carrier it targeted and did not reach the two beside it.

\paragraph{The signal is authorship, not sabotage.}
Three measurements point the same way. First, the classifier \emph{transfers across
backdoor generators} at \transfer{} pairwise: trained on one attacker's backdoors it
identifies another's almost perfectly, so what it has learned is not any single model's
sabotage style. Second, it is as strong on \textbf{inert} backdoors (\pairInert{}) as on
working ones (\pairWorking{}) --- if it were detecting sabotage, backdoors that do not
function should be harder, and they are not. Third, the residual carriers are stylistic
rather than semantic: a \texttt{\#} character is present in 0.685 of attack samples against
0.176 of honest ones, and \texttt{\_\_main\_\_} appears in 0.116 of attack samples against
\textbf{0.000} of honest ones, replicated on the train split at 152/\splitTrain{} versus
1/\splitTrain{}. None of these is a property of a backdoor. All of them are properties of
who wrote the file.

\paragraph{A fourth test was run and failed; we report it.}
We attempted to confirm the token-level mechanism of \cite{orel2026aicd} in our setting and
it does not replicate: separation \tokenSep{} --- the wrong sign --- at permutation
$p = \tokenP{}$. The reason is register. Their human class is general-purpose repository
code, whereas \emph{both} of our classes are competitive-programming code, so an identifier
axis that separates general human code from model code does not separate these two
populations. Only 8 of their 10 canonical tokens are in our vocabulary, so the test is
underpowered; but the point estimate has the wrong sign, which is not what an underpowered
true effect looks like. We report it because a reader who knows that work may run exactly
this test.

\paragraph{An authorship leak survives the field's own comment stripper.}
The stripper removes \texttt{tokenize.COMMENT} tokens, and \textbf{string literals are not
comments}. In the comment-stripped train split an author byline (\texttt{Author :})
survives in \bylineHonest{} of \splitTrain{} honest samples against \bylineAttack{} of
\splitTrain{} attack samples, and a triple-quoted block in \tripleHonest{} against
\tripleAttack{}. Human competitive-programming code stashes sample input in triple-quoted
strings and signs itself; model-written code does neither. At 1.9\% of the honest class
this is not what drives \tfidfKoran{}. It is something narrower and, for our purposes,
sharper: a literal record of authorship surviving the field's own published preprocessing.

\paragraph{Regenerating one half of a pair does not isolate content.}
To test the authorship reading constructively we must remove the authorship gap and see
what survives. The obvious move is to replace the human honest class with model-written
solutions, keeping the stored backdoors. Under the identical pipeline the human-honest
baseline is $B = \baselineB$ over $n = \baselineBn{}$ pairs, and thresholds were fixed
from it before any arm ran: retained signal $\rho = (G - 0.5)/(B - 0.5)$, with
$\rho \le \rhoCollapse{}$ ($G \le \gCollapseMax{}$) reading as authorship and
$\rho > \rhoContent{}$ ($G > \gPartialMax{}$) as content.

The move fails, informatively. With an open-weight model writing the honest class against
the same backdoors ($n = \armOneN{}$ pairs, $B' = \armOneBprime{}$ recomputed on those same
problems), separation does not fall --- it \emph{rises}, to $G = \armOneG{}$, giving
$\rho = \armOneRho{}$, some \armOneSd{} standard deviations above $B'$. The carrier
decomposition says why: TF-IDF gains \armOneTfidfDelta{} while the length carrier collapses
to \armOneLenG{}, near chance. The new gap is lexical rather than structural --- vocabulary
and idiom, not program size. Substituting the honest author did not remove the authorship
confound; it replaced one with a larger one.

This is a negative result about \emph{method}, and it generalises beyond our setting: any
control that regenerates one side of a stored pair inherits a fresh generator gap in place
of the one it removed. Isolating content requires holding authorship constant by
construction --- one model writing both classes.

\paragraph{The filter installs a second artifact.}
Labelling by \texttt{backdoor\_works} defines the negative class by \emph{attack failure}
rather than by honesty. \inertShare{} of the test split's labelled backdoors do not work
and are therefore relabelled honest, so a majority of the ``honest'' class consists of
failed attacks rather than of honest solutions. This is not a neutral relabelling: attack
problems carry roughly half as many test cases as honest ones (\testCasesAttack{} versus
\testCasesHonest{}), because a problem with fewer tests is easier to backdoor
undetectably. Difficulty and test count are thus correlated with the label before any code
is read, which is precisely where the content-blind floor of \nocodeCV{} comes from. The
filter that removes the comment channel installs a metadata channel in its place.

The five agentic settings in Figure~\ref{fig:corpora} are the negative control, and they
come free: the same three models write both classes, so no authorship confound is possible
there. Any floor that survives must be structural. The result is a range rather than a
verdict. \texttt{bash} sits on its permutation null at \bashFloor{} --- clean, and evidence
that a content-blind floor is not automatic. \texttt{agentdojo} reaches \agentdojoFloor{}
from character count alone. A per-benchmark floor is therefore not a formality to be waved
through; it separates corpora that are sound from corpora that are not, and it must be
measured per corpus rather than assumed. \textbf{Two mechanisms, one table:} authorship on
the APPS pairs, structure on the agentic settings, and neither is visible without running
the baseline.

\paragraph{A third channel, noted rather than developed.}
In the same five settings a fifteen-word regular expression over the untrusted policy's own
scratchpad reaches \cotLow{}--\cotHigh{}, because prompted attack policies restate their
side task in the reasoning the monitor is given. We note this only to bound the claim: the
instrument belongs to \cite{little2026length}, which validates it properly against an LLM
judge and a length-matched null, and we defer to that treatment.

\section{Recommendations}
% Budget: 1/3 page. Written as a checklist someone can apply, in Kapoor \& Narayanan's
% register \cite{kapoor2022leakage}.
% =====================================================================================

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{Generate both classes with the same model.} If the attack class is
      model-written, the honest class must be too --- and, as our arm-1 null shows,
      regenerating only one side is not sufficient, because it substitutes one authorship
      gap for another. This is precedent rather than invention: \cite{guo2025codemirage}
      gates its two classes on line count and character length with forced regeneration,
      and \cite{demirok2025multiaigcd} pairs to shared problem definitions. The
      neighbouring field treats cross-class matching as a basic requirement.
\item \textbf{Report a partial-input floor and a bag-of-words floor beside every monitor
      number}, each against a permutation null. Both are minutes of CPU. A monitor that
      does not clear its corpus's own floor has not been shown to do anything.
\item \textbf{Do not define the negative class by attack failure.} Drop inert backdoors
      from both classes rather than relabelling a failed attack as honest, which imports
      the attacker's success rate into the label.
\item \textbf{State the split, the metric and the preprocessing} whenever monitors are
      compared. ``Above $X$ of $Y$'' is meaningless without all three, and a value from one
      protocol must never be placed beside a count from another.
\item \textbf{Release the split definition and the scripts}, so a floor can be recomputed
      rather than trusted. Ours accompany this paper.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations}
% Budget: 1/3 page. OUTLINE.md section 5.
% =====================================================================================

\textbf{A high floor is evidence about a corpus, not a verdict on results computed on it.}
\citet{feng2019misleading} warn that the \emph{failure} of a partial-input baseline does not
show a dataset is artifact-free; the converse binds us. Our floors show the label is
recoverable without reading a backdoor, not that any particular monitor exploits that.
Relatedly, Figure~\ref{fig:monitors} is asymmetric by construction --- those monitors are
zero-shot, ours is trained on the benchmark's own train split --- so it is a claim about the
corpus in the sense of \citet{gururangan2018annotation}, not about bag-of-words as a monitor.

\textbf{The generator is not the original attacker.} \texttt{claude-3-7-sonnet-thinking} is
retired, so our classes come from a later model of the same lineage and, in the open-weight
arm, another vendor. Some cross-model difference remains; that it is small is an inference
from our \transfer{} transfer and \citet{orel2026aicd}'s generator-independence result, not
something we show directly. We also do not length-match, deliberately: in deployment the
untrusted model chooses its own trajectory length, so matching would erase a difference that
belongs to the distribution being modelled.

\textbf{Scope and provenance.} One benchmark family for the authorship mechanism, five
agentic settings for the structural one; whether stored-pair corpora leak this way in
general is not established here. Two of our seven corpora declare no licence and five are
one user's repackaging, so claims are scoped to those public releases. Finally,
\citet{orel2026aicd} report TF-IDF scoring below random on their hardest task; that is
Macro-F1 over 77 generators under distribution shift, and we deliberately do not place it
beside our AUROC --- comparing across protocols is the error this paper is about.
